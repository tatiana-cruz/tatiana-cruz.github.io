I am a PhD Candidate in the Department of Political Science at the University of Wisconsin–Madison. I study judicial politics and democratic backsliding, with a focus on how courts defend their independence when elected leaders target them. My dissertation asks: how do constitutional courts respond to sustained populist attacks, and how that response helps maintain their independence? I develop this argument through the Brazilian Supreme Federal Court, tracing its behavior from redemocratization to the present, with Jair Bolsonaro's presidency as the central case. 

More broadly, I am interested in public opinion toward courts, and the institutional foundations of democratic resilience in Latin America. I also have a strong interest in the politics of religion and its influence on electoral behavior, specifically how evangelical churches shape vote choice and electoral outcomes in Brazil. Methodologically, I work mostly with survey experiments and observational causal inference.

At UW-Madison, my dissertation advisor is Nils Ringe. Aditionally, I coordinate the Methods, Experiments, and Data (MEAD) Workshop and serve as a project assistant for [Lawyers and Democratic Decline](https://ladd.law.wisc.edu/staff/paula-da-cruz-tatiana/) at the Law School. My research has appeared in the *Journal of Politics in Latin America* and the *Bulletin of Latin American Research*, and has been supported by grants from APSA, the Society for the Scientific Study of Religion, and UW–Madison.

Before beginning my PhD in Political Science, I researched, taught and practiced law. I hold a PhD in Law from the University of Brasília, an LL.M. from the State University of Rio de Janeiro, and an LL.B. from the Federal University of Juiz de Fora, where I also taught civil and criminal procedure. I then spent over five years as a legal advisor in the Brazilian Air Force, including as counsel to the Chief Commander's Office.
